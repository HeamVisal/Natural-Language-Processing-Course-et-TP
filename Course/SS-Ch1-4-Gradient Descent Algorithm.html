What this guide covers

Source: §4 — Gradient Descent Algorithm, §4.1 — Stochastic Gradient Descent, and §4.2 — Training Neural Networks with PyTorch. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

Training means adjusting weights and biases so that a network’s predictions fit its data better. Gradient descent turns that goal into repeated numerical steps. This guide connects the geometry, the convergence proof, random mini-batches, and the lecturer’s cosine-training code.

What you will be able to do

  • Calculate a gradient-descent update by hand.
  • Explain the assumptions behind the convergence theorem.
  • Follow every step of its proof.
  • Read and adapt the 711-parameter PyTorch example.

How to study

Work through geometry and definitions before the proof. Then connect the proof’s ideal conditions to practical mini-batch training. Use the six labs to check your predictions.

Suggested pace: three sessions—geometry and assumptions; proof and SGD; code and experiments.

Gradient → learning rate → convexity → convergence proof → stochastic updates → tensor shapes → autograd → cosine training
Prerequisite Review

A parameter vector P stores all weights and biases. A loss f(P) assigns a number to their prediction error. A derivative measures local change; a partial derivative changes one coordinate while holding the others fixed. The gradient ∇f collects these partial derivatives. For a vector v = (v₁,…,vd), the Euclidean norm is ‖v‖ = √(v₁² + ··· + vd²); for example ‖(3,4)‖ = 5.

Prerequisite Review

Squared error at one input is (prediction − target)². Mean squared error (MSE) is the average over data points. In §3, the network was a function NNP; here we optimize its parameters P. The dimension d in f: ℝᵈ → ℝ counts optimization coordinates, which need not equal the network’s input dimension.

§4 · The update rule

Take a step downhill

Source: §4 — opening algorithm, learning rate, and surface illustration. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

Imagine standing on a smooth hillside in fog. You can feel the local slope even if you cannot see the whole landscape. Gradient descent uses that local information to choose a direction and take a step.

For a differentiable objective f: ℝᵈ → ℝ, the gradient ∇f(P) points in the direction of greatest local increase per unit Euclidean distance. Its negative points in the direction of greatest local decrease. It does not identify a distant global minimum.

Pk+1=Pk−η∇f(Pk)

Pk is the current parameter vector; k counts updates; ∇f(Pk) is the gradient evaluated there; η > 0 is the learning rate; and Pk+1 is the next vector. The algorithm is: choose P₀, compute the current gradient, subtract η times it, and repeat. All coordinates use derivatives at the same old point.

Worked Example · One variable

Let f(x) = x², so f′(x) = 2x. Begin at x₀ = 2 with η = 0.25.

  1. Find the slope

    f′(2) = 2 × 2 = 4. A positive slope says moving left initially decreases f.

  2. Scale and subtract

    x₁ = 2 − 0.25 × 4 = 1.

  3. Recalculate at the new point

    f′(1) = 2. Then x₂ = 1 − 0.25 × 2 = 0.5. Loss values are 4, 1, and 0.25.

Worked Example · Two coordinates

For f(x,y) = x² + 2y², the gradient is (2x, 4y). At P₀ = (2,1), it is (4,4). With η = 0.2, P₁ = (2,1) − 0.2(4,4) = (1.2,0.2). The loss falls from 2² + 2×1² = 6 to 1.2² + 2×0.2² = 1.44 + 0.08 = 1.52.

Why “downhill” needs a small step

The directional derivative along a vector v is ∇f(P)ᵀv. Along v = −∇f(P), it equals −‖∇f(P)‖², which is negative whenever the gradient is nonzero. This predicts a decrease for a sufficiently short move. The actual displacement is −η∇f(P), so its length is η‖∇f(P)‖. The learning rate is not itself the distance travelled.

For f(x) = x² from x = 2, η = 1 makes x₁ = −2, giving exactly the same loss 4. With η = 1.1, x₁ = −2.4 and the loss grows to 5.76. Even the correct local direction can overshoot.

Key Idea

Compute the gradient at the current parameters, subtract a scaled copy, and then recompute. A small local decrease is not automatically a global guarantee.

§4 · Definitions

The conditions behind a guarantee

Source: §4 — convergence theorem, convexity, strong convexity, Lipschitz definition, and flat-direction counterexample. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

The lecturer’s theorem describes a particularly well-behaved landscape: a bowl with one bottom and gradients that cannot change arbitrarily fast. These assumptions are mathematical conditions to check, not promises about every neural-network loss.

Convexity · The graph lies below each chord

f(λP+(1−λ)Q)≤λf(P)+(1−λ)f(Q),

P and Q are arbitrary points, λ lies in [0,1], and λP + (1 − λ)Q is a point between them. Convexity says the function at that intermediate point is no higher than the same weighted average of endpoint heights. For f(x) = x², P = 0, Q = 2, and λ = ½: f(1) = 1 ≤ ½f(0) + ½f(2) = 2.

For differentiable convex f, the tangent is a global lower bound:

f(P)−f(Q)≥∇f(Q)T(P−Q)

The right-hand term is the gradient at Q dotted with the displacement P − Q. For f(x) = x², Q = 1 and P = 3: f(3) − f(1) = 8 ≥ f′(1)(3 − 1) = 2×2 = 4.

Strong convexity · A quadratic lower bowl

f(P)≥f(Q)+∇f(Q)T(P−Q)+μ2‖P−Q‖2.

μ > 0 is a single lower-curvature constant that works for all P,Q. The extra term μ‖P − Q‖²/2 forces growth away from the tangent. For f(x) = x², μ = 2 works: at Q = 1 and P = 3, the lower bound is 1 + 2×2 + (2/2)×2² = 9, equal to f(3).

Lipschitz continuity · Which function is controlled?

‖F(P)−F(Q)‖≤L‖P−Q‖

This definition applies to a map F: ℝᵈ → ℝᵈ. L > 0 bounds how much its output can change relative to input distance. In the theorem, F = ∇f: the gradient must be Lipschitz, meaning ‖∇f(P) − ∇f(Q)‖ ≤ L‖P − Q‖. This is often called L-smoothness of f. It does not mean f itself is globally Lipschitz.

Again take f(x) = x². Its gradient is 2x, so |2P − 2Q| = 2|P − Q| and L = 2 works. Between P = 3 and Q = 1, the gradient changes by |6 − 2| = 4, exactly 2 times the input distance 2.

The theorem stated explicitly

Assume f is differentiable, strongly convex with constant μ > 0, bounded below, nonconstant, and has a globally Lipschitz gradient with constant L > 0. Then f has a unique global minimizer Pc. For a fixed learning rate satisfying 0 < η < 2/L, the sequence Pk+1 = Pk − η∇f(Pk) converges to Pc from any P₀. The proof is next.

Notation Clarification

The source writes Df: ℝᵈ → ℝᵈ. Strictly, the derivative Df(P) is a linear functional on displacements. In Euclidean coordinates it is represented by the gradient vector ∇f(P); that is the Lipschitz map used throughout the proof. We keep the source’s ∇f notation.

Why convex and smooth do not give uniqueness

The source’s counterexample is f(x,y) = x². Its gradient (2x,0) is globally 2-Lipschitz, and it is convex. Every point (0,y) minimizes it, so the minimum is not unique. Moving in the y direction changes no loss. This is a flat direction and prevents strong convexity with any μ > 0.

For the lab’s f(x,y) = ½(x² + ay²), a ≥ 1, we can use μ = 1 and L = a. With a = 4, the theorem permits 0 < η < 0.5. Taking η = 0.5 can leave the y coordinate oscillating forever, which explains the strict inequality.

§4 · Complete reasoning

Follow the convergence proof

Source: §4 — proof of the convergence theorem; existence and uniqueness filled in as Extra Explanation. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

There are three separate claims to justify: a unique minimum exists; the gradient norms tend to zero; and the parameter points themselves approach that minimum. Decreasing losses alone prove none of the last two claims without additional reasoning.

Extra Explanation · Why the unique minimum exists

The source leaves this part to the reader. Strong convexity with Q = 0 gives

f(P) ≥ f(0) + ∇f(0)ᵀP + (μ/2)‖P‖² ≥ f(0) − ‖∇f(0)‖‖P‖ + (μ/2)‖P‖²

The second inequality uses Cauchy–Schwarz, |uᵀv| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖. Since μ > 0, quadratic growth eventually dominates the possibly negative linear term. Thus f(P) → ∞ as ‖P‖ → ∞, a property called coercivity. Outside a sufficiently large ball the values exceed f(0). Inside that closed bounded ball, continuity ensures a minimum is attained. It is therefore a global minimum.

At a differentiable unconstrained minimizer Pc, ∇f(Pc) = 0. If another minimizer Q existed, strong convexity would give f(Q) ≥ f(Pc) + (μ/2)‖Q − Pc‖². Equal minimal values force the squared distance to be zero. Hence Q = Pc: the minimizer is unique.

1

Control a step using the Lipschitz gradient

The smoothness or descent inequality is

f(Pk+1)≤f(Pk)+∇f(Pk)T(Pk+1−Pk)+L2‖Pk+1−Pk‖2.

The linear term predicts local change, and the quadratic term limits the error in that prediction. More generally, for Δ = Q − P, the extra error is ∫₀¹[∇f(P + tΔ) − ∇f(P)]ᵀΔ dt. Lipschitz continuity and Cauchy–Schwarz bound it above by ∫₀¹Lt‖Δ‖²dt = (L/2)‖Δ‖². This explains the inequality used by the source.

2

Substitute the gradient-descent displacement

Here Pk+1 − Pk = −η∇f(Pk). Its dot product with ∇f(Pk) is −η‖∇f(Pk)‖², and its squared norm is η²‖∇f(Pk)‖². Substitution gives

f(Pk+1)≤f(Pk)−η‖∇f(Pk)‖2+Lη22‖∇f(Pk)‖2.
f(Pk+1)≤f(Pk)−(η−Lη22)‖∇f(Pk)‖2.

Write c = η − Lη²/2 = η(1 − Lη/2). This coefficient is positive exactly when 0 < η < 2/L. The bound becomes f(Pk+1) ≤ f(Pk) − c‖∇f(Pk)‖². Values strictly decrease unless the gradient is zero.

Numeric check: for f(x) = x², L = 2, x₀ = 2, η = 0.25: c = 0.25 − 2×0.25²/2 = 0.1875. The gradient is 4, so f(x₁) ≤ 4 − 0.1875×16 = 1. The actual x₁ is 1, so its loss equals this bound.

3

Decreasing values have a finite limit

The sequence f(Pk) is nonincreasing and bounded below. Therefore it converges to a finite number mf. At this point we have not yet proved mf equals the minimum, and we have not proved Pk converges.

4

Sum the decreases: a telescoping bound

0≤(η−Lη22)∑k=0N‖∇f(Pk)‖2≤f(P0)−f(PN+1)≤f(P0)−mf

The sum runs from k = 0 to an arbitrary integer N. Intermediate function values cancel, leaving the initial value minus the last value. The right-hand side f(P₀) − mf is a fixed finite bound independent of N. Since c > 0, the infinite series of nonnegative numbers ∑‖∇f(Pk)‖² converges. Each term must tend to zero, so ‖∇f(Pk)‖ → 0.

For the x² example above, gradients are 4, 2, 1, ½, … . Their squared sum is 16 + 4 + 1 + … = 16/(1 − ¼) = 64/3. Multiplying by c = 3/16 gives 4, exactly the initial loss minus its limit 0.

5

Use strong convexity in both directions

We now use the already established Pc and ∇f(Pc) = 0. Apply strong convexity once from Pc to Pk, and once in the reverse direction:

f(Pk)≥f(Pc)+μ2‖Pk−Pc‖2,f(Pc)≥f(Pk)+∇f(Pk)T(Pc−Pk)+μ2‖Pk−Pc‖2.

Adding cancels the function values and combines the two μ/2 terms:

μ‖Pk−Pc‖2≤∇f(Pk)T(Pk−Pc)≤‖∇f(Pk)‖‖Pk−Pc‖.

The second inequality is Cauchy–Schwarz. If Pk = Pc, the distance is already zero. Otherwise divide by the positive norm ‖Pk − Pc‖ to obtain

‖Pk−Pc‖≤1μ‖∇f(Pk)‖→0,

Because the gradient norm tends to zero and μ is fixed and positive, the distance tends to zero. This proves Pk → Pc. Continuity then also gives mf = f(Pc).

A practical meaning for the final bound

If μ = 2 and ‖∇f(P)‖ ≤ 0.01, the proof gives ‖P − Pc‖ ≤ 0.01/2 = 0.005. This is a distance guarantee only under the strong-convexity assumptions. A small gradient for a general neural-network loss does not supply it.

Common Mistake

“The loss decreases and is bounded below” proves convergence of the numbers f(Pk). The telescoping argument and the strong-convexity estimate are the additional steps that prove the points converge to the unique minimum.

§4 · Interpreting the theorem

What the guarantee does not cover

Source: §4 — irregular surface and warning about typical machine-learning objectives. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

The theorem is a conditional guarantee. If its assumptions do not hold, the proof does not apply. A nonconvex objective may have several minima, stationary maxima, saddle points, or flat directions.

A global minimum is no higher than every other value on the domain. A local minimum is no higher than nearby values. A stationary point has zero gradient; this can be a minimum, a maximum, or neither. The source’s surface animation shows how a path can depend on the landscape and its starting point.

Worked Example · Stationary at the wrong place

For f(x) = (x² − 1)², f′(0) = 0 and f(0) = 1. Starting at x₀ = 0, every gradient-descent step remains exactly at zero. Yet f(1) = f(−1) = 0. A zero gradient alone did not identify the smallest loss.

Starting instead at x₀ = 0.4 with η = 0.1 gives f′(0.4) = 4×0.4×(0.16 − 1) = −1.344. Thus x₁ = 0.4 − 0.1(−1.344) = 0.5344, moving toward the right-hand well. A negative starting value yields the reflected trajectory.

Neural networks also have symmetries: swapping equivalent hidden neurons can preserve their function. The resulting parameter-space objective need not have a unique minimizer. Strong convexity is therefore an ideal teaching case, not a general description of the cosine network trained later.

Quick Summary

A suitable step size is necessary even for a smooth bowl. Outside the theorem’s assumptions, reducing loss is useful progress but does not prove convergence to a global minimum.

§4.1 · Stochastic gradient descent

Train using smaller random batches

Source: §4.1 — empirical loss, mini-batch algorithm, batch size, epoch example, and convergence remark. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

Computing every data point’s contribution before each update can be expensive. A mini-batch gives a cheaper, noisy estimate of the full gradient, allowing more frequent parameter updates.

The source assumes an unknown target function F and observed pairs D = {(x₁,y₁),…,(xN,yN)} with yᵢ = F(xᵢ). The architecture fixes P ∈ ℝᴷ. Its empirical objective is

lossD(P)=∑i=1N|F(xi)−NNP(xi)|2N=∑i=1N|yi−NNP(xi)|2N

N counts examples; yᵢ is an observed output; NNP(xᵢ) is the network prediction. In real datasets labels may contain measurement noise, but the lecturer’s cosine example has exact targets. Differentiating an average gives the average of its gradients.

∇lossD(P) = (1/N) ∑i=1N ∇P|yᵢ − NNP(xᵢ)|²

For a mini-batch D̃ of size n, average only its n errors and subtract its gradient:

lossD̃(P) = (1/n) ∑(xᵢ,yᵢ)∈D̃ |yᵢ − NNP(xᵢ)|²; Pk+1 = Pk − η∇lossD̃(Pk)
  1. Choose a random mini-batch

    Its data provide one estimate of the full-data objective and gradient.

  2. Compute its loss and gradient at the current P

    Use an average over the batch so the scale is comparable when n changes.

  3. Update P and repeat with another batch

    The batch changes from step to step. Batch size 1 is classical single-sample SGD; intermediate sizes are mini-batch SGD; n = N gives a full-batch gradient.

Worked Example · A noisy but unbiased gradient

For the lab’s constant prediction w and one target y, the loss is (w − y)² and its derivative is 2(w − y). At w = 0 the four gradients are 4, 0, −4, and −8. Their average is (4 + 0 − 4 − 8)/4 = −2, equal to the gradient 2(w − 1) of the full MSE.

If the first sampled target is −2, η = 0.1 gives w₁ = 0 − 0.1×4 = −0.4. The full MSE increases from 6 to (−0.4 − 1)² + 5 = 6.96. A single stochastic update need not reduce the full loss, even when its expected gradient points correctly. A full-batch step would give w₁ = 0.2 and full MSE 5.64.

Epoch arithmetic · a separate shuffled-pass example

The tiny simulation is not a neural-network trainer. It uses a repeatable seed so resets are comparable. Its random batches do not define exhaustive epochs. The epoch calculator instead assumes a shuffled partition that keeps the last partial batch.

Batch size, updates, and epochs

The source’s example has N = 1000 and n = 20, so a single exhaustive pass partitioned into batches takes 1000/20 = 50 updates. This pass is one epoch. If N is not divisible by n and the final partial batch is kept, use ⌈N/n⌉. For N = 103, n = 20, there are five full batches and a final batch of 3: six updates.

Source Clarification · Random draws are not automatically an epoch

Independently choosing random batches can revisit some examples and miss others. Exactly 50 such choices need not cover all 1000 examples. The source’s arithmetic describes an exhaustive partition. Its PyTorch loop samples single indices with replacement; 40,000 draws from 200 points equal 200 dataset-sized draw budgets, not 200 guaranteed complete passes.

What is and is not guaranteed?

Uniformly sampled mini-batch gradients are unbiased estimates of the full empirical gradient at a fixed current P. Their individual directions still vary. The deterministic proof in §4 does not transfer unchanged to stochastic updates. Constant-rate SGD may continue fluctuating around a minimizer; its convergence needs additional conditions on sampling, noise, the objective, and the learning-rate schedule.

Source Clarification · Overfitting is not automatically solved

The lecture introduces SGD as addressing computational cost and overfitting. Smaller updates are cheaper, and stochasticity can affect generalization, but using random mini-batches does not guarantee that a network will avoid overfitting. Performance on held-out data still matters.

Worked Example · Why broadcasting can silently go wrong

For predictions [[1],[2],[3]] and targets [1,2,3], the shapes are [3,1] and [3]. Broadcasting subtracts every target from every prediction, producing a 3×3 array. Squared errors sum to 12 and their mean is 12/9 = 4/3, even though corresponding predictions and targets agree exactly. With targets [[1],[2],[3]], the residual shape is [3,1] and the MSE is 0. §4.2 · Loss, backward, and updates

Read the training loop correctly

Source: §4.2 — original code blocks 4–5 and explanation of zero_grad. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

There are three different operations to keep apart: evaluate a prediction, differentiate the loss, and change the parameters. The original loop implements these explicitly.

4 · Define the single-example loss and learning rate

Original lecturer code · block 4 of 6Python · run outside this HTML
#Define loss function
def loss_fn(a,b):
    return (a-b)**2
#choose learning rate
lr=0.01

def defines a function; return provides its result; **2 squares the difference. For a one-element prediction tensor, this loss has one element, so backward() can infer its initial gradient. For a real multi-example batch, use a scalar reduction such as ((a-b)**2).mean(), with matching target shapes.

5 · Sample, predict, differentiate, and update

Original Code Notice

The next block is preserved from the lecturer, including param.data -= lr * param.grad. The adapted version below uses an in-place update directly on the parameter under torch.no_grad(), avoiding .data. The mathematical SGD update is the same.

Original lecturer code · block 5 of 6Python · run outside this HTML
# Start gradient descent step
for _ in range(40000):
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(x), (1,)).item()      # select a random index between 0 and size of x
    input=x[idx:idx+1]                               # get the point x corresponding to the index selected above
    output=y[idx]                                    # get the value of cosine 
    predicted_out=net(input)                         # value computed by the neural network
    loss=loss_fn(predicted_out,output)               # compute the error 
    net.zero_grad()                                  # make the gradient associate to parameter in net to be zero
    loss.backward()                                  # compute the gradient for any parameter used when computing loss
    with torch.no_grad():                            # stop tracking gradient
        for param in net.parameters():
            param.data -= lr * param.grad
            
# Now evaluate how the model changed after training
with torch.no_grad():
    predicted_by_net=net(x.view(-1,1))
plt.figure(figsize=(12,3))        
plt.plot(x,y,label='Cosine')
plt.plot(x,predicted_by_net,label='NeuralNet')
plt.xlabel('Input')
plt.ylabel('Output')
plt.title('Cosine vs NeuralNet')
plt.legend()
  1. Select an example

    torch.randint(0,len(x),(1,)) creates a one-element integer tensor with index 0,…,199; the upper endpoint is excluded. .item() converts it to a Python integer. The name input in the original is just a variable; it shadows Python’s built-in function, so the adapted code uses xb.

  2. Make a prediction and a loss

    The source input has shape [1], target is a scalar, and output has shape [1]. Scalar-to-one-element broadcasting is harmless here. net(input) runs the forward pass. The loop creates a new computation graph each time.

  3. Clear old gradients

    net.zero_grad() resets the model’s stored parameter gradients before differentiating the new loss. This is essential because gradients accumulate across backward calls.

  4. Differentiate

    loss.backward() uses the chain rule through the network and accumulates a derivative in each participating leaf parameter’s .grad. It does not change the parameter values.

  5. Perform the update

    Inside torch.no_grad(), each parameter subtracts lr * param.grad. These update operations are not recorded as part of the next differentiation graph. They leave the stored gradients present until cleared.

  6. Evaluate the fitted curve

    x.view(-1,1) reshapes 200 elements into [200,1]; −1 asks PyTorch to infer the first size. The network then evaluates all 200 points at once. The plot compares targets with trained predictions.

Source Clarification · None versus zero

The source describes .grad as initially zero and zero_grad as always filling it with zeros. For leaf parameters it is normally None before the first backward; Module.zero_grad(set_to_none=True) also resets it to None. This differs from an allocated zero tensor, but both prevent old derivatives being added into the next intended gradient. See the Module.zero_grad documentation.

Worked Example · The accumulation error

For l(w) = (w − 3)² at w = 1, the derivative is 2(1 − 3) = −4. Backward stores −4. With η = 0.1, the update gives w = 1 − 0.1(−4) = 1.4, but the buffer remains −4.

Recomputing the loss at w = 1.4 gives fresh derivative 2(1.4 − 3) = −3.2. Without clearing, the buffer becomes −4 + (−3.2) = −7.2. Using it yields w = 1.4 − 0.1(−7.2) = 2.12. Clearing first would instead use −3.2 and produce w = 1.72. Those are different algorithms.

    Adapted complete example · same SGD taskPython · run outside this HTML
    import torch
from torch import nn
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

torch.manual_seed(7)  # Repeatable initialization and index draws.
x = torch.linspace(-10, 10, 200).view(-1, 1)
y = torch.cos(x)  # Both x and y have shape [200, 1].

class NeuralNetwork(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.A1 = nn.Linear(1, 40)
        self.A2 = nn.Linear(40, 15)
        self.A3 = nn.Linear(15, 1)

    def forward(self, values):
        hidden1 = torch.tanh(self.A1(values))
        hidden2 = torch.tanh(self.A2(hidden1))
        return self.A3(hidden2)

net = NeuralNetwork()
lr = 0.01
assert sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters()) == 711

def training_mse():
    with torch.no_grad():
        return ((net(x) - y) ** 2).mean().item()

print("Initial training MSE:", training_mse())
for step in range(40000):
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(x), (1,)).item()
    xb = x[idx:idx + 1]  # [1, 1]
    yb = y[idx:idx + 1]  # [1, 1]
    net.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    prediction = net(xb)
    loss = ((prediction - yb) ** 2).mean()
    loss.backward()
    with torch.no_grad():
        for parameter in net.parameters():
            if parameter.grad is not None:
                parameter.add_(parameter.grad, alpha=-lr)

print("Final training MSE:", training_mse())
wide_x = torch.linspace(-20, 20, 1000).view(-1, 1)
with torch.no_grad():
    train_prediction = net(x)
    wide_prediction = net(wide_x)
assert train_prediction.shape == y.shape
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(13, 3))
axes[0].plot(x.squeeze(), y.squeeze(), label="Cosine")
axes[0].plot(x.squeeze(), train_prediction.squeeze(), label="Network")
axes[0].set_title("Training interval")
axes[1].plot(wide_x.squeeze(), torch.cos(wide_x).squeeze(), label="Cosine")
axes[1].plot(wide_x.squeeze(), wide_prediction.squeeze(), label="Network")
axes[1].axvspan(-10, 10, color="green", alpha=0.08)
axes[1].set_title("Wider interval; shaded region was trained")
for axis in axes:
    axis.set_xlabel("Input x (radians)")
    axis.set_ylabel("Output")
    axis.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

    Interpolation concerns new inputs within the covered range; extrapolation concerns inputs outside it. Matching a finite grid does not guarantee even interpolation at every intermediate point, and it certainly does not certify correct extrapolation.

    Why this finite tanh network cannot fit cosine uniformly on all ℝ

    The lecturer explains failure using a finite-critical-point claim. A safer argument for this exact architecture uses limits. Each first-layer unit tanh(ax + b) has a finite limit as x → +∞: 1 if a > 0, −1 if a < 0, or the constant tanh(b) if a = 0. Finite weighted sums, another tanh layer, and the linear output preserve a finite limit. Thus the network output tends to some constant c.

    Cosine has no such limit: at x = 2πj it equals 1 and at x = (2j + 1)π it equals −1 for arbitrarily large integers j. The limiting errors along these two sequences are |1 − c| and |−1 − c|; at least one is ≥ 1. Therefore no fixed finite network of this tanh architecture can achieve uniform error strictly below 1 over all ℝ. This does not prevent very accurate approximation on any chosen compact interval.

    Source Clarification · Keep the claim tied to the architecture

    “A neural network has finitely many critical points” is not a general explanation: even a constant network has infinitely many stationary points, and activation choices matter. The finite-limit argument above establishes the intended limitation for this finite tanh network without relying on that claim. It also avoids reading the lecturer’s interpretation of “learning” as a formal theorem about understanding.

    What the heat maps measure

    Each cell is a parameter value, with sign encoded by colour and magnitude by intensity. The matrix norm used in the lecture is the Frobenius norm, the square root of the sum of squared entries:

    ‖W‖=∑ijWij2

    Wᵢⱼ is the value in row i and column j; the sum includes every entry. For a bias vector, the same idea gives its Euclidean norm. A one-entry bias has norm equal to its absolute value.

    Worked Example: for W = [[1,−2],[0,2]], the squared-entry sum is 1² + (−2)² + 0² + 2² = 9, so ‖W‖F = 3. A larger norm does not by itself mean a better model: loss depends on the whole network’s function.

    Review, glossary, and source notes

    Source: §4, §4.1, and §4.2 — all selected content. Added examples and labs are teaching supplements.

    Quick Summary

    • The update: Pk+1 = Pk − η∇f(Pk).
    • The guarantee: strong convexity, a Lipschitz gradient, and fixed 0 < η < 2/L yield convergence to the unique minimizer.
    • The proof: establish a unique minimum → bound each decrease → telescope → show gradient norms vanish → bound distance by gradient norm/μ.
    • The stochastic version: average a randomly chosen mini-batch’s gradients. Individual steps can increase full loss.
    • The PyTorch loop: form loss → clear gradients → backward → update under no_grad. Each action has a distinct role.
    • The example: 200 points on [−10,10], two tanh hidden layers, 711 parameters, η = 0.01, and 40,000 single-example draws.
    • The evaluation: low training MSE is not a proof of good extrapolation or a global-minimum certificate.

    Glossary

    Gradient ∇f
    Vector of partial derivatives at the current optimization point.
    Learning rate η
    Positive factor multiplying the gradient in an update.
    Stationary point
    A point with zero gradient; it need not be a minimum.
    Global minimizer P_c
    A point whose objective value is no greater than at any other point.
    Convex function
    A function lying below every chord between two graph points.
    Strong convexity μ
    A uniform positive quadratic lower-curvature bound.
    Lipschitz gradient / L-smoothness
    A bound ‖∇f(P) − ∇f(Q)‖ ≤ L‖P − Q‖ for all points.
    Coercivity
    The objective tends to +∞ as the point’s norm tends to infinity.
    Telescoping sum
    A sum in which consecutive positive and negative terms cancel.
    Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
    The dot product magnitude is at most the product of Euclidean norms.
    Empirical loss
    An average error measured on a finite dataset.
    Mini-batch
    A group of examples used for one loss and update.
    SGD
    Gradient updates using randomly selected examples or mini-batches.
    Epoch
    One complete pass through the training dataset.
    Tensor shape
    The size of each axis of a tensor, such as [200,1].
    Broadcasting
    Automatic expansion of compatible tensor dimensions in an operation.
    Autograd
    Automatic differentiation through a recorded computation graph.
    Gradient buffer .grad
    Storage into which backward accumulates parameter derivatives.
    no_grad
    A context that disables backward-graph recording for its operations.
    Tanh
    A smooth activation with range (−1,1) and finite limits at ±∞.
    Frobenius norm
    The square root of the sum of all squared matrix entries.
    Interpolation / extrapolation
    Prediction within / outside the input range represented by the training data.
